How to become dangerously good at selling (use these 3 strategies)

Most creators will never make a full-time living from it.

You can be the best in the world at something, but if nobody knows who you are, what you do, or what value you bring, nobody will know about it, and you’ll end up being a starving artist.

Most creators are starving artists.

A starving artist is someone who is great at what they do but never learned marketing, sales, and persuasion to turn their knowledge, interests, and skills into a living.

They use their inability to make money as virtue signaling.

“Look at me. Look at how pure my work is! Look at what true art looks like!”

You’ll see them complain on social media that their work comes from the heart.

But who said that making money from your work means it’s any less valuable?

Let’s get one thing clear:

There’s nothing noble about being a starving artist.

It doesn’t make your work more authentic.

If anything, it makes your work less authentic because you are now creating from a place of scarcity. And your refusal to learn how to turn it into a profit leaks into your work. You become more resentful of those who are making it their full-time job, doing what they love for work, and this all changes your personality, the way you act, create, think, and behave.

I used to wear the starving artist as a badge of honor. In 2024, I didn’t want to “taint” my work by selling under my brand. But what ended up happening was that I still needed money to survive. So I became a ghostwriter, writing tweets for $250 a pop. But only this time I wasn’t selling my work, but I was selling other people’s work.

It was only when I was on vacation that I realized the problem I created for myself. I was in the best hotels, in the nicest area, with the best shops, but I spent all of my vacation in my hotel researching, learning, and writing about childhood trauma for a client. I didn’t have any interest in writing about childhood trauma.

This made me hate writing.

So the only way for me to make a living doing something I love, brings me meaning and fulfillment, was to sell my own products because I genuinely believe in them and they are aligned with what I’m trying to build.

If you’re not selling your own products, you’re selling someone else’s.

The only authentic way to do work you love for a living is to sell the work you actually believe in.

This letter is about how to market your work and to make a meaningful living doing so.

If you think that marketing and selling is evil and that it’s only for greedy corporations that want to secretly destroy the world for the sake of profit, do yourself a favor and stop reading.

But if you’re one of the few who want to make content creation their full-time job. To make a living doing something you love, to have complete control over your time, income, and freedom, keep reading.

With that said, here are 3 ways to sell more of your products:

## 1) Open, Close, Refine, Launch (Beginner)

Humans are too scared to lose out on opportunities.

It’s the reason why Adam ate the apple from the forbidden tree, or why people buy a new TV during Black Friday (even though they bought one last year), or why you go out even when you know you won’t enjoy it.

Humans want to experience everything. We can’t help it. This is why productivity porn is also becoming a thing. You want to be more productive so you can do everything at once.

With marketing, you can leverage this trait to help your audience to solve their problems.

Because to get people to buy your products that help them solve their problems, you need pressure.

2 ways to add pressure:

- Urgency — a reason for people to move now. Discounts, limited-time offers, etc.
- Scarcity — limited quantity. Limited stock, only a few spots, etc. The idea behind this method is pretty simple.

You open your product for a few days (5-7), people can buy during that time, then you close it for 2-3 months.

Once the doors are closed, your goal is to work closely with your customers to improve your product, implement their feedback, iterate on your product, then open it again. You can either launch it at the same price again, or increase it.

And once the second launch is over, you have a decision to make:

Either turn your product into an evergreen product (people can always buy it), or close it again and repeat the same process.

Where you can use this method:

- Digital Products - you can’t make digital products scarce (they’re digital), but you can add urgency by not making it available all the time.
- Workshops - you can run people through your process in the workshop, and post-workshop, you can collect feedback.
- Cohorts - cohorts have clear start and end dates. Once the cohort starts, it doesn’t make sense for someone to join in the middle.
- Communities - you can stop accepting new members to focus on the members you already have, refine the material that’s inside, overdeliver to those who are already inside, collect social proof, and launch it again either at the same price or a higher price. I use this method 90% of the time and the reason is simple:

It’s my first time building that product.

When it’s your first time selling your product, it’s best to let your product go through a few rounds of iteration. Refine your process so your results are more consistent.

This also allows you to collect more testimonials which help improve your product and conversions.

## 2) Tripwire Method (Intermediate)

Not everyone will buy from your launch.

Some people are more skeptical. And it’s fair. Social media is a sketchy place. Skepticism is at an all-time high, and trust is at an all-time low.

Giving people unconditional value in the form of content, lead magnets, or free training helps build the trust between you and your audience.

It shows that you’re the man for the job and you mean serious business.

It also has a nice benefit because you get to collect people’s email addresses (which helps build your email list).

A tripwire offer needs 3 things:

- A free resource - this is the thing that will “hook” your audience in. It can be a lead magnet or a free masterclass.
- An email sequence - this can be a 10, 20, or 30-day email sequence.

## 3) 4-Week Launch (Advanced)

Think of a launch like an elastic band.

The longer and harder you pull, the bigger the snap.

A 4-week launch plan gives you more marketing firepower to play with.

What you can do during the 4-week launch:

- Launch a giveaway - giveaways get you a huge influx of leads (from my experience, I got 100-200 leads each time).
- Manual outreach - you can reach out to previous customers, leads, and interested people in your audience and have conversations and close them on a call or in the DMs.
- Gradual price increase - start the launch with a 40% discount, and each week, the prices increase by 10% until you reach full price. If you’re doing the gradual price increase, you don’t need to be sending daily emails for 4 weeks, because you’re going to have something called Offer Fatigue.

Offer fatigue means your audience got sick of your offer and your offer isn’t converting as much as it used to.

The best approach is to do 2-3 emails per week (1 newsletter, 1 storytelling email + CTA at the end, and 1 reminder that prices increase tomorrow) leading up to full price.

That’s what I did at the beginning of the year and I made $7,000 from my cohort (it was my biggest launch at the time).

The most underrated part about this strategy is that you can create customers.

How?

By educating them through your newsletters about their problems, your vision, and your narrative.

Raise their awareness levels with your long-form newsletters and when you do so, your readers will grow to want your product.

In general, there are 5 awareness stages:

![Image](https://pbs.twimg.com/media/HU7JD_BbkAA2RCM?format=png&name=medium)

[X.com media](https://x.com/HussainIbarra/article/2112198119672959413/media/2111846345577566208)

- Problem unaware - unaware of their problems and how it’s impacting their day-to-day life (focus on pain points).
- Problem aware - aware of their problems but don’t know how to solve them (focus on the impacts of the pain points and your unique point of view).
- Solution aware - aware of their problems and know that solutions exist, just not a streamlined solution (focus on actionable advice).
- Product aware - aware of their problems, know that there is a streamlined path, system, or solution that exists (focus on your unique mechanism).
- Most aware - they are ready for change, they just need a reason to act now (focus on objections and adding pressure like urgency and scarcity). Your goal leading up to launch week is to educate your audience on their problems and show them that your product is the solution.

The 4-week plan (what to do in each week):

Week 1:

99% of your audience are beginners.

This week, your newsletters will be beginner-focused and more general when it comes to your topic.

You can use copywriting frameworks like PAS, PAS(O), or AIDA to structure your newsletter.

As you’re writing your newsletter, soft-promote your product. You can write it in the middle and/or at the end.

You can write something like this:

> “Quick announcement:
>
> If you want to [HEAVEN], my product, NAME, is currently on a 40% discount until Sunday, then prices increase.
>
> You can get it here: LINK.”

When it comes to social media, go to your high-performing post of the day, and link that week’s newsletter under it.

On Sunday, send another email to your list reminding them that in 24 hours prices increase by 10%. It doesn’t need to be too long. The shorter and more straight to the point, the better.

Week 2:

Very similar to Week 1. Not much will change here.

Week 3:

You will turn the marketing engine on.

You will still do what you did in Weeks 1 and 2. But this week, you’ll launch a lead magnet.

The goal of this lead magnet is to give people the general framework of your product.

You will not actually give them a step-by-step plan on how to implement your product.

What you will do is talk about the overall framework from a big-picture POV.

Give them the what and why, but keep the how.

Once the lead magnet is done, do a “giveaway” post. Ask people to reply to your post and you’ll send it to them.

When you’re sending them the lead magnet, write a short message telling them about your product and if they want to learn more about it, they need to reply with a keyword.

You can use Eden to automate and customize your DMs so you don’t have to spend all day sending people your lead magnet.

Week 4:

In this week, you will question reality.

From my experience, launch week is where the majority of the sales happen.

You see, a launch is like a train. In the beginning, you’re not sure if what you heard was a train or not. But as it gets closer, the train gets louder, and the people know for sure what’s coming.

During launch week, your goal is to be make as much noise as possible (on email and social media).

Start sending short, daily emails telling people to buy and that doors close on a certain date. On social media, write a daily post telling people to buy your product.

Your audience should feel the scarcity and that something big is happening this week.

“But Hussain, I don’t want to annoy my audience.”

Do you genuinely believe in the product you’re selling?

If so, then promoting it and getting as many people to buy it is an act of service. If you care about helping people and making the world a better place, you need to learn how to market, sell, and persuade people to buy your products.

On the last day, you will make so many sales, you wouldn’t believe it (I usually make 30-50 sales on the last day).

> Rule of Thumb: The more emails you send on the last day, the more sales you’ll make.

Personally, I like to do 3 reminder emails (24 hours left, 12 hours left, 3 hours left).

Once doors close, take the product down and focus on delivery.
